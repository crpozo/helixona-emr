Flags
What a flag means, who it is for, and how loudly it says it
A flag has a category — who cares about it — and a severity — how loud it should be. They are separate on purpose: an admin flag can be urgent, and a medical one can be a footnote. Only high severity rings the block on the calendar.

A flag also has a lifetime. A pregnancy ends after nine months and a missing consent should stop shouting once it is signed; a documented food allergy never expires. An expired flag is not deleted — it stops showing on the calendar and stays on the record, because the fact that it was once true is itself clinical history.

Appointment patterns & insights
Measured from arrival to finished — and who ran it
The measure is the door, not the booking. A pattern is built from how long the patient actually took — walked in to line out, walked in to sample labelled — against what was held for them. An infusion is not run by a chair, it is run by the person who started it, so the pattern is read against Bea, Nick or Juan and against the treatment. They last whatever they last, and the point of measuring is to hold the right amount of time rather than the round number.

If it cannot be measured, no insight is offered. A row appears here only when there are enough completed visits with the same person and the same treatment to say something. Everything else stays silent — a guess dressed as a pattern is worse than an empty list, because somebody will book against it.

How the alert works
A slot frees
Somebody cancels, no-shows, or a provider block is lifted. The schedule notices the moment it happens — nobody has to remember.
The list is matched
Type and subtype must match. Then the desired windows are checked, then how long they have waited. Only real matches are offered.
The slot is held, briefly
Whoever is offered gets 30 minutes before it passes to the next match. The slot stays visibly held on the calendar meanwhile.
